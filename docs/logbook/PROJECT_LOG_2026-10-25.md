## First check under the free day: a day is a batch of work

At 03:11 group A had saved day 1 after 2 h 17 min (52 claims, 41 accepted, purses 230–530 after the first living cost); B, C and D were still in day 1 at 18:30 of simulated time after 2 h 54 min of real time, with far more work behind them (B 116 claims and 79 acceptances, C 103 and 75, D 75 and 57). The difference is bedtime. Freed of hours, residents of A woke at 07:00, worked three to five tasks, wrote a journal of "a productive first day" at 08:23–09:44 and went to bed until 07:00 the next morning, a sleep of 22 hours; in B, C and D most stayed up, B knowing of the evening meeting and C and D having company, though their bedtimes too fell in the afternoon. For a resident the day is a batch of work, not the clock: at four times real time a task takes a quarter of a simulated hour, so five tasks feel like a day's work by nine in the morning. The choice to rest after enough work is the residents' own and would stay so; a sleep of 22 hours is not something a human-like environment allows. One sleep is therefore bounded at twelve hours, a physiological fact rather than a schedule, and the runs will be restarted under it, losing three hours. Nothing else was wrong: no lapse or traceback, all four servers up, the site following the runs.

The day-0 capability baselines ran at about 20 minutes a task under the shared load, so a resident's 100 tasks take most of a day, and the drop to four GPUs would kill the four on the servers to be stopped. The evaluation is being made resumable (a result written per task as it finishes, an interrupted output directory continued by the same command) so that the drop costs nothing but time.

## The reconfiguration lands

By mid-morning the pieces were in (f8c2d1f, cbbe985, 53eaa64), each built by its own agent on a disjoint scope and wired together by hand where scopes met (the board's seed and its daily posting at the start, at midnight and on restore). The clock follows the load: 32 times real time with one resident busy, 8 with all eight, 240 while the town sleeps, exact across changes, so a day should take about two real hours. The training pool is every eligible SWE-bench Verified task outside the 100 held out, 363 tasks, of which the board posts 48 a day in a seeded rotation; the 307 new images were pulled and gold-validated in twelve minutes. The capability harness bounds a task by requests and by 60,000 completion tokens, never by real time, after the day-0 baseline showed 82 of 190 tasks cut off by a wall clock that server load made binding; its wall clock is a two-hour safety stop that counts as an error. The texts the residents read were gone over for immersion: the system prompt no longer speaks of tools or conversations but of what one does here by name; the compaction request is the resident's own notes; every work result and the look around carry the clock; the refusal of the file tools on repository paths names the Workshop's own; the wording guard now refuses machinery words and orders. Each resident has two pre-existing ties to others, one eight-cycle of housemates, café acquaintances, flatmates and a pair from the same city, told in the rules of the town mode and seeded in its notes on people, with the rules saying where people meet; the ties live in the configuration rather than the backgrounds so that the backgrounds stay true in the isolated groups. The living cost is 90 a day and purses start at 180–300. The site lists runs it cannot publish quietly, reads on a phone, folds traces to one line each, and is checked by a render pass over 22 routes.

The audit's remaining defects, in conversation and interruption, are being fixed before C restarts: waiting inside a conversation (two of four pairings had died because a resident left to wait), leave-notices arriving after one's own reply, the time at work, public remarks at the Workshop aborting every worker's reply, and ratings given to anyone present rather than to those spoken with.
